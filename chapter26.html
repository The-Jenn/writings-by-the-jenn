Chapter 26

Betrayal

Lisa stood at the kitchen island, cutting an apple. She sliced it into thin, even wedges, arranging them on a small plate next to a turkey sandwich.

It was one o'clock on a Saturday afternoon. The house was quiet.

For the first time in weeks, Lisa actually felt good. The panic that had been running her life had calmed a bit.

Knowing she had Carla, knowing they were in this together, something felt different.

She picked up the plate, turning toward the hallway to bring Becky her lunch.

As she walked down the hallway she heard crying. She stopped. She stood still, listening.

It was Becky.

"I just don't know what I'm doing wrong, Bets," Becky's voice drifted through the open bedroom door.

Lisa frowned, taking a step closer to the door. Betsy had gone in there ten minutes ago to drop off some clean laundry.

"Mom, don't cry," Betsy said. She sounded incredibly stressed, the tone of a teenager forced to parent an adult. "She's just tired. She's working and taking care of you."

"No, it's more than that," Becky cried. "You don't see how she looks at me when you aren't in the room. She looks at me like I'm a chore. Like I'm this huge burden she has to drag around. She doesn't touch me. She barely talks to me."

Lisa’s grip on the plate tightened.

"She's just stressed," Betsy pleaded, trying to rationalize it, Becky sounded uncomfortable.

"I think she wants to leave us," Becky whispered. "I really do, Betsy. I think she's just waiting for me to be able to walk again so she can pack her bags and walk out the door. She doesn't love me anymore. I'm broken, and she doesn't want a broken wife."

"Please don't say that," Betsy's voice cracked, upset now.

"I'm sorry, baby," Becky sobbed. "I shouldn't put this on you. You're just a kid. I just... I'm so scared. I'm stuck in this bed and I can feel her slipping away, and there is nothing I can do to stop it. If she leaves us, Betsy... what are we going to do?"

Lisa stood frozen in the hallway.

Becky wasn't just manipulating Lisa anymore. Because Lisa had stopped acting like a frantic, guilty mess, because Lisa was acting calm and composed, Becky had recognized the loss of control and immediately pivoted to the one thing Lisa loved more than anything in the world.

She was weaponizing their own daughter. She was intentionally terrifying a sixteen-year-old girl just to tighten the leash on Lisa.

The bedroom door suddenly opened.

Betsy walked out, holding an empty laundry basket against her hip.

Her face was flushed, with tears in her eyes. She nearly ran right into Lisa in the hallway.

Betsy stopped. She looked at the plate of food in Lisa’s hands, then looked up at Lisa's face. There was no anger in the teenager's eyes, only a deep sadness that made Lisa's chest ache.

"Betsy, hey," Lisa said softly, keeping her voice gentle.

Betsy just looked at her. Her chin trembled slightly. "Mom... are you going to leave us?"

The words hurt. Lisa felt a sharp ache in her throat. She wanted to pull her daughter into a hug and tell her the truth. She wanted to tell her that she thinks her mom didn't slip on those stairs, that she did it on purpose, that she was a monster holding them all hostage.

But she couldn't.

"No," Lisa said, stepping forward and setting the plate on edge of the small hallway table.

She reached out, resting her hands on Betsy's shoulders. "Look at me, Bets. I am not leaving you. I promise you, I am not going anywhere."

Betsy looked at the floor. "She thinks you are. And you just seem so far away lately. I don't know how to fix it."

"You don't have to fix anything," Lisa said, squeezing her shoulders. "It's just been a really hard month. We're all just tired."

Betsy gently pulled out of Lisa's grip. She set the empty laundry basket on the floor, wiping her cheeks with the sleeves of her shirt. "I just need some air."

"Okay sweetie," Lisa whispered.

Betsy turned and walked to the front door, pulling it open and stepping out into cool day.

The calm Lisa had found that morning vanished, replaced by anger.

She turned around, walked down the hallway, and pushed the guest room door all the way open.

Becky was sitting propped up against the pillows. Her eyes were dry. The pathetic crying woman from three minutes ago was gone.

Becky looked at Lisa, her expression blank.

Lisa walked into the room and closed the door.

"What the hell did you just do?" Lisa asked.

"I don't know what you mean," Becky said. She reached over with her uninjured hand and adjusted the blanket over her chest.

"Where is my lunch? I'm starving."

"Do not play games with me right now," Lisa said, stepping toward the bed, stopping at the edge.

"I heard you. I heard what you told her. You are trying to use our daughter to manipulate me."

Becky looked up. "I was just being honest with her. She's old enough to understand what's going on in her own house. She can see you pulling away."

"I am not pulling away," Lisa said. "I am doing everything you ask. I am bathing you, I am feeding you, I am sleeping in a chair next to this bed."

"Physical care is not a marriage," Becky said coldly. She let her head tilt back against the pillows. "Your body is here, but you aren't. You go through the motions like a robot. You don't hold my hand unless I ask you to. You don't look at me like you love me."

Becky’s eyes met Lisa’s . "What are you thinking about when you sit in that chair, Lisa? Who are you thinking about?"

"I'm thinking about how to pay the hospital bills," Lisa lied, her face a mask of exhausted frustration. "I'm thinking about how I have to call the precinct on Monday and beg the Chief to let me take another week of desk duty so I can stay home. I'm thinking about the fact that I haven't slept more than three hours a night in a month."

Becky watched her. The silence between them was uncomfortable.

"You're so angry," Becky finally said. "You're angry that you got stuck with me."

"I am angry that you are using Betsy to make yourself feel better," Lisa yelled. "She is sixteen years old. She doesn't need to be carrying the weight of your insecurities. If you have a problem with how I'm taking care of you, you tell me. You do not cry to her."

"She's my daughter, too," Becky said, the smile fading into something much darker. "And she knows who has her best interests at heart. She knows who is actually fighting for this family."

Lisa stared at her. She was acting like a monster.

“I am going to get your sandwich," Lisa said, her voice dead of any emotion.

She turned around and walked out of the room. She walked back into the kitchen, and leaned against the table, trying to figure out how to put out the fire Becky had just started in Betsy's head.

Outside, the October breeze made Besty cold. She was walking fast, trying to keep warm. She didn’t grab a jacket.

She wiped at her tears. Her chest felt tight, with anxiety. She couldn't stand the way her mother cried in that bed, and she couldn't stand the hollow, exhausted look on her other mother’s face.

She reached the property line between their yard and the Carlas's house, walking toward the corner of the street.

"Betsy?"

The voice came from the left. Betsy stopped, and looked over.

Carla was standing by her mailbox, a stack of envelopes in her hand. Carla looked at the teenager, sad face and tears, the crossed arms, and the lack of a jacket.

Betsy quickly wiped her face again, looking away, embarrassed. "Hi, Carla."

She stepped away from the mailbox and walked down the driveway, stopping a few feet from the sidewalk. She didn't know what had happened over there, but it was obvious the kid was falling apart.

"Hey," Carla said gently. "Are you alright? It’s chilly out."

Betsy shook her head, unable to speak at the moment.

She wrapped her arms tighter around her own chest, shivering. Carla didn't ask her to explain. "Come inside. It's freezing out here. Let me make you a brew."

Betsy hesitated for a second, looking back toward her own house, but the thought of going back in was too much.

She nodded and followed Carla up the driveway.

Carla opened front door and let Betsy step into the warm house. She pointed to one of the tall stools at the kitchen. "Sit. I'll get the kettle going."

Betsy sat down, pulling her knees up to her chest. She watched Carla move around the kitchen, pulling two mugs from the cabinet and filling the electric kettle.

"Here," Carla said, sliding a box of tissues across the counter.

"Thank you," Betsy said, pulling one out and blowing her nose.

Carla leaned against the counter opposite her, waiting for the water to boil. She didn't push. She just waited.

"I'm sorry.." Betsy said, staring down at the tissue in her hands. "Everything is just... a mess right now."

"Don’t be sorry. What's going on?”

Betsy let out a nervous breath. "It's just them. My moms. It's just awful in there. I can't breathe. My mom Becky... she's just so sad. She cries to me, telling me that mom hates her now because she's broken. She thinks she wants to leave us."

Carla felt anger, but she kept her face perfectly neutral.

"And when I asked my Mom about it just now," Betsy continued, her voice cracking, "she just looked so tired. She promised she wasn't leaving, but she just seems so quiet. So far away."

"It's hard to watch two people you love go through a crisis," Carla said carefully. She couldn't bash Becky directly, or Betsy would close up. "Accidents like that... they change the whole dynamic of a house. Your mom is in a lot of pain, so she's scared. And Lisa is a cop. She's used to being able to fix things, and she can't fix a broken bone. Taking care of someone around the clock takes a massive toll."

The kettle went off. Carla turned, dropping two tea bags into the mugs and pouring the boiling water over them. She slid one mug across to Betsy.

Betsy wrapped her cold hands around the warm mug, letting the heat soak into her skin. She looked at Carla.

"But it isn't just the stress," Betsy said. "My mom Becky... she's acting weird. Not just sad. Weird."

Carla went still. She took a slow sip of her tea, then asked. "Weird how?

Betsy shifted uncomfortably on the stool, looking down into her tea. "Like she's playing a game. Yesterday, mom ran out to get the mail, and I was in the kitchen. I walked down the hall to ask Mom if she wanted the TV on. The door was cracked."

Betsy paused, looking guilty for even saying it out loud.

"What did you see, Betsy?" Carla asked, keeping her voice gentle, recognizing exactly how important this moment was.

"She was reaching for a book," Betsy whispered. "On very top shelf of the bookcase.
